A

Two things the photos changed

Before any of the vocabulary: the photo set moves two things the requirements are built on. The first narrows the fan choice; the second cuts the scope. Both are worth settling before P1 is modelled.

Finding 1 — constrains the fan choice

The fan fits, but it will struggle to breathe

Measured at the wall: 50 mm of clearance under the sill in the dining room, 100 mm in the living room. So the top-mounted fan of §3 survives — 100 mm is comfortable, and even 50 mm swallows a 25 mm fan plus a collar and a seal with a few millimetres spare.

The problem moves from fit to intake. An axial fan wants roughly a quarter of its diameter of free space on the suction side to draw evenly — about 30 mm for a 120 mm fan. In the dining room the stack leaves only 10–15 mm of plenum under the sill, so the fan inhales through a slot instead of an open hemisphere. That costs flow and, worse for G4, makes noise: a starved axial fan whistles.

This is a fan-selection problem, not a geometry problem, and it has a tidy answer — see section E.

Finding 2 — scope

Three of the seven are not panel radiators at all

The requirements assume Type 11/21/22/33 panel radiators throughout, each with a top slot and a convector channel to duct into. The main bathroom has a towel rail, the second bathroom a vertical tube radiator, and one niche holds an old sectional radiator. None of them has a top slot or fins. P1 as conceived cannot mount to any of them.

There is also an open identification question on the two long low units. They may be panel radiators — or they may be convector casings: a finned tube inside a sheet-metal box with a full-length grille on top. If it is the latter, the front or top cover probably lifts off, and fans could sit inside the casing blowing up through the grille, which sidesteps Finding 1 entirely. Section D covers what to look for.

B

Reading the type number

"Type 22" is not a model name — it is a two-digit description of the cross-section. The first digit counts the water panels, the second counts the convector fin banks welded between and behind them. So Type 11 is one panel with one set of fins; Type 33 is three of each, stacked front to back.

This matters because it tells you whether an air channel exists at all. On a Type 22 the two fin banks face each other with a gap down the middle — that gap is what channel_gap in measurements.md refers to, and it is what a top-mounted fan would blow into. On a Type 11 there is no such gap; air simply passes through the single fin bank.

C

The C-numbers and P-numbers

These are just labels from the requirements document so that later sections can refer back without repeating themselves. The C-list is constraints — things the design is not allowed to break. The P-list is the parts to be designed.

Constraints

C1
Condensation is already handled. The heat pump raises coolant temperature to stay above dew point. Fans must not defeat that — and they mostly can't, since forcing air over fins barely changes fin surface temperature.
C2
No modification of the radiator. Mounting by magnets on the painted steel, plus hooking over the top edge. No drilling, no adhesive.
C3
Windowsills limit height above the radiator. The mount must be low profile. This is Finding 1 — it may be tighter than "low profile" can absorb.
C4
Parts stay mounted year-round. So they see heating-season surface temperatures. PLA creeps around 55–60 °C, so print in PETG or ASA, and leave clearance for thermal expansion rather than relying on press fits.
C5
Winter bypass. Blanking the top slot for the fans would also block the natural convection chimney the radiator needs in winter. Preferred fix: reverse the fans to blow upward in the heating season.
C6
Standard 120 × 120 × 25 mm fans, 105 mm screw pattern.
C7
Daisy-chained to one 12 V supply per radiator, ideally switched from the cooling signal.

Parts

P1
Fan hanger / top mount. Holds one fan over the convector channel and seals it to the top slot. The keystone part — it fixes every interface dimension, so it gets designed first.
P2
Blanking tile. Closes the top slot between the fans so air can't take the easy way out instead of going down the channel. Printed end clips carrying cut PVC foam or aluminium sheet.
P3
Discharge deflector. Turns the downward flow into a horizontal jet at floor level. Sets the room circulation loop and stops the jet blowing straight onto ankles.
P4
Cable clip. Trivial, printed entirely.
D

Why the part has the shape it has

The whole geometry follows from one decision in §3: the fan sits on top and blows down. Not because of buoyancy — at 0.12 Pa the buoyant head is nothing against 20–50 Pa of fan pressure — but because of intake air temperature. The warmest air in a room is at the top. Feeding that to the radiator maximises the air-to-water temperature difference, which is the whole point.

The bonus is the room loop: cold air leaves at the floor, crosses the room, rises at the far wall, returns along the ceiling, and is consumed by the fan intake. The return leg terminates at the intake rather than wandering off, so the loop reinforces itself.

Two consequences fall straight out of this, and they are why P2 and P3 exist at all: the top opening has to be sealed everywhere except at the fans, or the air spills straight back out; and the discharge has to be turned sideways, or you get a cold draught on the ankles.

E

What P1 looks like, in principle

The material strategy in §6 is what gives the part its character: print only the interfaces. Large flat areas and long curves are cut from sheet stock; the printed part is just the bracket that holds a fan at an exact position relative to the radiator's top edge, and seals the gap between them.

So P1 is small. It is a fan-sized collar with three jobs — locate the fan, seal to the metal, and hold itself on without screws — and almost nothing else.

The one change the photos force

The requirements describe P1 as having a "duct collar down into the slot". IMG_7181 is the only frame where a ruler actually crosses the fin banks, and it puts the clear channel at roughly 10–15 mm. You cannot duct a 120 mm fan into a 12 mm slit — the collar would be a knife edge, it would foul the fins on the way in, and it would make the part depend on a dimension you cannot hold.

The alternative is to stop trying to reach the channel and simply seal onto the flat top surface of the grille, letting the grille itself spread the flow along the slot. The seal length goes up, the part gets simpler and more tolerant, and the mount no longer cares what the channel gap is. The cost is that bypass now depends entirely on P2 doing its job.

Living with 50 mm: pick the fan, then the bracket

With 100 mm of headroom the living room takes an ordinary 25 mm fan and nobody has to think about it. The dining room's 50 mm is the case that sets the design, and the lever is fan thickness rather than bracket cleverness — every millimetre saved off the stack goes straight into the intake plenum.

Three moves, in the order I would take them:

  • Use 15 mm slim fans. A 120 × 120 × 15 costs perhaps 20 % of the pressure of a 25 mm fan at the same speed, but it hands back 10 mm of plenum — and here the plenum is what is actually throttling you. Net gain.
  • Drop the finger guard on the tight radiator. C6 wants one, but nothing reaches into a 50 mm gap under a stone sill. That is another 3–4 mm.
  • Run more fans, slower. The fin pack is the dominant resistance, so flow scales better with fan count than with RPM — and many slow fans are markedly quieter than few fast ones, which is exactly what G4 asks for. A 2 m radiator has room for seven or eight.

If that still is not enough, the honest alternative is to stop using axial fans on the tight radiator and use a tangential (crossflow) blower instead — the long thin roller found in fan-assisted radiator products. It is the right shape for a 2 m slot, it is happy with a restricted inlet, and it is quiet at low speed. It costs more, needs a different bracket family, and cannot be reversed for the winter trick in C5, so it is a fallback rather than a default.

Can a 120 mm fan even sit there? Depth says: only sometimes

Headroom was the vertical question. Depth is the horizontal one, and it turns out to be the harder of the two. A fan sitting flat on the radiator discharges over a 120 mm circle; the radiator can only accept that air across the width of its top face. If the fan is wider than the radiator is deep, the surplus blows at the sill, the wall and the room — it does no work at all.

With the three depths now measured, the four panel radiators fall into three quite different cases:

  • Dining room, 135 mm — the easy one. A 120 mm fan sits entirely within the top face, and the grille is about as wide as the fan. No transition needed, which is lucky, because with 50 mm of headroom there is no room for one.
  • Living room, 100 mm — workable. A 120 mm fan overhangs by about 10 mm on each side. Let it overhang towards the room rather than the wall, and add a short tapered plenum to gather the discharge onto the grille. The 100 mm of headroom pays for that plenum comfortably.
  • Office, 60 mm — a 120 mm fan is the wrong part. The fan would be twice as wide as the radiator is deep, with roughly two-thirds of its output missing the grille entirely. Either drop to 60 mm fans, which are noisy for the air they move, or use a tangential blower, whose roller is naturally 60–80 mm across and exactly the right shape for a long narrow slot.

Note how the two constraints pull in opposite directions across the house: the radiator with the least headroom has the most depth, and the one with the most awkward depth is the one whose headroom is still unmeasured. There is no single fan choice that suits all four.

F

Does six fans mean six times the cooling?

Nearly, but only if you spread them out. The answer splits cleanly in two, and the distinction matters more than the diminishing returns everyone expects.

Fans over new radiator length add up almost linearly. A fan only forces air through the section it sits over; the rest of the radiator carries on with feeble natural convection. So the second fan does not help the first one's patch — it activates a fresh patch that was previously doing almost nothing. Six fans spread along two metres give close to six times one fan's contribution.

Fans over the same patch saturate fast. Air-side heat transfer scales roughly with velocity to the power 0.6–0.8, so doubling the air over one spot buys about 1.6× that spot's heat transfer, not 2×. Stack fans on one section and each one is worth less than the last.

There is a real ceiling underneath both, but it is further away than you might think. The radiator cannot move more heat than the water carries through it: Q_max = ṁ · cp · (T_room − T_supply). At a typical circuit flow that cap sits several times above where six or seven fans land, so you are still working in the near-linear region — the curve has barely started to bend. That is also why the return-water temperature actually rises when the fans come on, which is the whole point of the exercise.

The practical rule falls out of this: space the fans evenly and use as many as the length allows, rather than concentrating a few powerful ones. It happens to serve G4 too, since many slow fans are quieter than few fast ones.

I

The measurement list

In priority order, with headroom now struck off. The first item decides whether the project's core geometry survives, so it is worth doing before anything else. Each says where to find the feature, using the photos as the reference.

  1. Does the top grille or front cover lift off? On the two long low units, try lifting the full-length grille and pulling the front panel forward off its clips. If either comes away, fans can go inside the casing and the headroom problem disappears. Also settles whether these are panel radiators or convector casings.
  2. Channel gap, all four panel radiators Lay a caliper's jaws down through the top slot until they touch the fin tips on both sides. Not the sheet-metal slot above it — those differ. In IMG_7181 this is the dark band running between the two rows of wavy fins. Decides FIG 4: duct in, or seal on top.
  3. Slot width in the sheet metal Rule flat across the top, measuring the opening in the pressed steel — the feature you see edge-on in IMG_7163 and IMG_7166. This sets the collar's footprint if FIG 4's right-hand option wins.
  4. Headroom — office and guest WC The two that were not measured. Same method: rule standing on the radiator top, read off where the sill underside crosses. The guest WC may have no sill at all, in which case it is unlimited and the easiest of the four.
  5. Overall length and height, each radiator Tape measure along the wall, and floor to top edge. These drive fan count and the P2 tile lengths; they do not need to be precise to the millimetre.
  6. Depth of the guest WC unit, and wall standoff on all four Depth is now known everywhere except the guest WC — rule across the end cap, as in IMG_7176. Then the gap from each back face to the wall, which sets how far the magnet skirt can reach round. Depth decides the fan diameter, so the guest WC one is a real gap.
  7. Floor clearance Floor to the bottom edge. In IMG_7178 and IMG_7179 the units look like they nearly reach the skirting, which would rule out a bottom-mounted deflector (P3) as well as any fallback to upward flow.
  8. Tube diameter and pitch — the two bathrooms Only needed if the tube radiators stay in scope. Caliper across one tube, then centre-to-centre between two adjacent ones.
On re-shooting

The room constraints are real and more photos will not fix most of these — a rule photographed at an angle in a tight corner will always be hard to read. Measure by hand against this list instead, and use the photos only to confirm you are looking at the same feature I am.
